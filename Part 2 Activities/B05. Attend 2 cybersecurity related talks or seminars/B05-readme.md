# B5. Attend 2 cybersecurity related talks/seminars.

Attending these two cybersecurity events was a very useful experience for me because both of them helped me understand the industry from a much more practical point of view. In both sessions, I was not just learning concepts from class or reading about cybersecurity online. I was actually there, listening to professionals speak, hearing their experiences, and understanding how the field looks in real life. What I found especially valuable is that the two events complemented each other well. One of them gave me stronger insight into industry pathways, networking, and the diversity of careers in cybersecurity, while the other gave me a more direct look into what employers and professionals expect, what real cyber careers involve day to day, and how students can prepare more effectively for entering the field. Together, these events made cybersecurity feel much more real, professional, and connected to the wider industry community.


## 1. Students of Cyber May Event hosted by CyberCX

The first event I attended was the Students of Cyber May Event hosted by CyberCX. I found this to be a very meaningful session because it brought together students who were genuinely interested in cybersecurity and gave us the opportunity to hear directly from professionals already working in the field. The event had a very strong atmosphere. The room was full, people were engaged, and it was clear that there was a lot of interest in learning not only about cybersecurity itself, but also about how to actually build a future in the industry. That immediately made the session feel valuable to me, because it was not just a talk where people came to sit quietly and leave. It felt like a proper community event where students were there to ask questions, make connections, and understand what the cyber industry really looks like from the inside.

One of the things I appreciated most about this event was the range of perspectives shared by the speakers, including Billy Hunter, Liam O’Shannessy, and Natasha Ferguson. A key message that really stayed with me was the idea that there is no single pathway into cybersecurity. I found this especially encouraging because students often feel pressure to have a perfect plan or a very fixed route into the field, but this event showed that the industry is much broader than that. Different backgrounds, different experiences, and different strengths can all have value in cybersecurity. That made the session feel honest and realistic, rather than making the field seem narrow or only accessible to a certain type of person. It also made me think more seriously about how cybersecurity includes not just technical skill, but also adaptability, curiosity, communication, and the willingness to keep learning.

Another important part of the session was hearing Liam O’Shannessy give an early preview of the 2026 Hack Report. I found this especially interesting because it made the event feel connected to current and real cybersecurity issues rather than staying only at the level of general career advice. It showed that the session was also engaging with active industry concerns and current threat discussions. That gave the event more depth and made it feel more professionally grounded. Overall, I found this talk very worthwhile because it combined industry insight, community building, career perspective, and current cyber discussion in a way that was both engaging and useful. It helped me feel more connected to the cybersecurity space and gave me a stronger sense of how students can begin positioning themselves within it.

## 2. Operation Wasp: Cyber Industry Night by Curtin Cyber Security Club

The second event I attended was Operation Wasp: Cyber Industry Night, organised by the Curtin Cyber Security Club. This was another very valuable cybersecurity event because it brought together students and industry professionals in a setting that felt direct, practical, and very career-focused. What stood out to me straight away was that the event had a strong turnout and a serious level of interest from the people attending. It felt less like a casual student gathering and more like a real industry engagement night where students had the chance to hear unfiltered perspectives from professionals working in different parts of cybersecurity. That was one of the things I liked most about it, because it created a space where students could learn about the field in a more realistic way rather than only hearing polished or idealised versions of cyber careers.

The speaker lineup was one of the biggest strengths of the event. The session featured professionals such as Mark Calleja from MaltIQ, Jevon Ang from Huntress, Benjamin Ockwell from McGrathNicol, Shanil Daya from Ever Nimble, and Jorel Paddick from CyberCX. I found this especially useful because it meant the event offered more than one view of the cybersecurity industry. Instead of hearing from only one company or one kind of role, we were exposed to people with different experiences and likely different areas of focus across the field. That made the session feel broader and more balanced, and it helped me understand that cybersecurity is not one single job but a wide professional space with many directions, expectations, and ways of working.

What I found most valuable about this event was how honest and grounded the discussion felt. The speakers did not only talk about exciting parts of the field or give generic motivational advice. They spoke about what a career in cybersecurity really looks like, how people actually break into the industry, what employers are genuinely looking for, and what the day-to-day realities of the field can involve. That made the session much more useful for me as a student, because it connected cybersecurity with the practical questions that many students actually have: how to get started, how to become employable, and what skills or mindset matter most once you begin. The audience questions also added a lot to the event, because they made the conversation feel more interactive and showed that many other students were thinking seriously about similar concerns.

Another thing that made Operation Wasp stand out was the networking aspect. The event did not feel like it ended the moment the formal speaking stopped. It felt like the conversations, questions, and connections carried on beyond the main session, which made it much more valuable as an industry event. To me, that is one of the biggest strengths of cybersecurity talks like this: they do not only provide information, but also create opportunities for students to interact with the industry in a much more direct way. Overall, I found Operation Wasp to be a very meaningful event because it gave me realistic exposure to the cybersecurity profession, helped me understand employer expectations more clearly, and reinforced how important it is for students to attend these kinds of sessions if they want to build confidence and direction in the field.
