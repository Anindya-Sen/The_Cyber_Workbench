# B1. Discover 5 unique weak/vulnerable security implementations.

## 1. Easy tailgating into controlled spaces

One unique weak security implementation I discovered was easy tailgating through the controlled entry gate at the ground floor of UWA’s Reid Library after normal operating hours. I only started noticing this more carefully because I had been staying in the library late recently to study for the upcoming semester final exams, and one night it caught my attention. There is clear information around the library explaining that access becomes restricted to the general public after standard hours, which shows that the university has already tried to put a proper access-control system in place. The idea behind it is actually strong in theory: students are supposed to scan their student ID card to unlock the entry gate, so only authorised users should be able to enter. However, what I observed that night was that after one person scanned their card and opened the gate, two or three other people followed directly behind them without scanning anything themselves, entering before the gate had time to close again. That made me realise that this is a weak security implementation not because there is no control at all, but because the system fails to verify each individual person entering the premises. In other words, the security measure depends too heavily on the assumption that only one person will pass per scan, and that assumption can be broken very easily in practice. After seeing this, I understood that the weakness here is a form of access-control bypass through tailgating, where the gate mechanism works, but the real-world implementation is not strict enough to stop unauthorised entry during busy or distracted moments. This fits the activity well because it was something I physically noticed myself, it made me think more deeply about how access control works in real life, and it showed me that even a good security idea can still become vulnerable if the implementation does not properly enforce one-person-at-a-time entry.

## 2. Unattended logged-in computer in a shared area
Another weak security implementation I discovered was an unattended logged-in computer in a shared public area. I recently noticed a computer on the first floor of UWA’s Reid Library that was open and still logged in under a student’s session, even though the student was not there. It is possible that the student had only stepped away briefly, for example to go to the toilet or get food, but even a short absence creates a real security risk when the device is left accessible in a public or semi-public place. What made this stand out to me is that the computer itself may not look suspicious at all, but it is actually a very weak implementation of device and session security because anyone with bad intentions could interfere with the account, access personal files, view private information, send messages, or make changes while pretending to be the original user. After observing this, I understood more clearly that a logged-in device is not safe simply because it belongs to the real user. If it is left open in a shared environment, the protection offered by passwords or identity-based login is effectively weakened, because the security only works properly while the user is physically controlling the device.

## 3. Sensitive screens visible to bystanders
A third weak security implementation I discovered was sensitive screens being openly visible to bystanders. I noticed this at the IT Help Desk reception area on the ground floor of UWA’s Reid Library, where staff-operated desks are positioned quite openly near the entrance. The screens are not turned away from public view or shielded in any meaningful way, so someone walking past can often see what is on the display without even trying very hard. That made me realise this is a weakness in the way confidentiality is being protected in a digital workspace. Even if staff are using secure systems properly, the visible placement of the screens means information could still be exposed to people nearby. Depending on what is being displayed at the time, that could include internal account information, service requests, names, emails, technical details, or other sensitive content. What I found important here is that the weakness is not necessarily in the software itself, but in the way the digital system is physically used in a public-facing space. It showed me that cybersecurity and privacy are not only about passwords or technical controls, but also about whether digital information is being displayed in a way that keeps it out of sight from unauthorised people.

## 4. Westpac’s trust-based customer verification in banking scams
One unique weak security implementation I discovered was the use of trust-based customer verification in banking scams, which I came across through a Westpac scam alert on Facebook. The post warned customers that scammers were impersonating Westpac or other banks and trying to convince people to do things that a real bank would never ask for, such as leaving a bank card or cash in a letterbox for collection, sharing online banking security codes, installing software that gives remote access to a phone or computer, or helping with fake “sting operations” to catch hackers. What made me curious was that all of these scam methods depend on the same underlying weakness: people are being tricked into treating a message, caller, or instruction as legitimate without properly verifying who is really behind it. After looking into it more, I understood that the vulnerable implementation here is not a broken lock or faulty software feature, but a weak security process where identity is accepted too easily through appearance, branding, urgency, and persuasion. If someone believes the scammer is really from the bank, then they may willingly hand over codes, install remote-access tools, or follow unsafe instructions themselves, which completely bypasses the normal technical protections of the account. In that sense, the weakness is a form of social-engineering-based authentication failure: the security breaks because trust is granted too quickly without independent verification. I found this to be a very strong example for this activity because the post made me realise that some vulnerable security implementations do not come from advanced hacking at all, but from weak real-world verification practices that allow attackers to manipulate users into defeating their own security.

## 5. AusPost public scam alert
One unique weak security implementation I discovered was the way fake parcel-delivery phishing websites take advantage of people’s trust in well-known services such as Australia Post. While scrolling through Facebook, I came across an alert shared by 9 News Adelaide about a recent Australia Post scam, and that immediately caught my attention because these kinds of parcel messages feel very normal and easy to believe, especially when so many people are regularly expecting deliveries. That made me curious, so I went home and researched the issue more deeply and found that the real weakness is not only the fake message itself, but the weak security implementation in how users verify websites and links before entering sensitive details. In these scams, attackers create messages and fake websites that closely imitate a trusted company, and if the user clicks the link without properly checking the URL or authenticity of the page, they may hand over personal, payment, or login information to the attacker. What I found especially interesting is that this weakness sits between technology and human behaviour: the phishing page is designed to look convincing, but the real security failure happens when appearance and urgency are treated as proof that the site is legitimate. This made me understand that one vulnerable implementation in everyday digital life is relying on visual trust and brand familiarity instead of proper verification, because once that happens, a fake website can bypass the user’s judgment very easily. After researching it further, I learned that this kind of weakness is dangerous because it turns an ordinary action, such as checking a delivery message, into a point where private information can be stolen simply because the security decision is being made too quickly and too casually.
