# B14. Teach your friends about cybersecurity topic of your choice.


For this activity, I chose to teach my friends about how fake internship, job, and recruitment scams work. I specifically chose this topic because it is highly relevant to students and young adults like us, especially at a stage where many of us are actively searching for internships, part-time roles, graduate opportunities, and industry connections. I live with two close friends, and I have often noticed them discussing job advertisements and online opportunities that sounded very attractive, but sometimes also a little unrealistic. They would occasionally ask me what I thought about certain messages, offers, or job links, especially when the offer looked too good to be true. Because of that, I felt this would be a very useful topic to sit down and discuss properly with them, not just as a casual warning, but as a real cybersecurity conversation that could help them avoid being tricked in the future.

When I spoke to them, I focused on the way these scams are designed to look professional, exciting, and believable. I explained that fake recruitment scams often use polished language, company logos, recruiter-style messages, and urgent timelines to create the impression that the opportunity is genuine. I told them that these scams can come through email, LinkedIn, WhatsApp, Telegram, or even regular-looking job advertisements, which is why they can be difficult to notice immediately. I then walked them through the most important warning signs, such as very high pay for vague work, unusual pressure to act quickly, requests to move the conversation away from official channels, and demands for personal information or money before any real interview or hiring process has taken place. I especially stressed that no genuine employer should be asking students to pay for training, onboarding, software, or processing fees in order to “secure” a role.

I also taught them how to protect themselves in a practical way. I explained that if they ever receive a job offer unexpectedly, they should first verify whether the recruiter is real, whether the email address actually belongs to the company, and whether the job is listed on the official organisation’s website. I also told them to look closely at whether the company has a genuine digital presence and whether the communication feels consistent and professional. Most importantly, I reminded them never to send passport details, banking information, verification codes, or payments just because someone is claiming to offer an opportunity. The main lesson I wanted them to take away was that a real job opportunity should survive proper checking, so there is never a need to rush into giving away sensitive details. I found this activity very meaningful because it was directly relevant to my friends’ lives, and I felt that the discussion genuinely helped them become more careful and more aware of how these scams target students specifically.
