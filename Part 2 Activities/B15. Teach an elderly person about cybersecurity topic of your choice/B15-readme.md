# B15. Teach an elderly person about cybersecurity topic of your choice.


For this activity, I chose to teach an elderly person about how scammers use urgency, fear, and authority to manipulate victims, and how those manipulation tactics can be recognised and resisted. The person I taught was a family friend in Bangladesh who runs a small electronics store, so I felt this topic was especially appropriate for him. I chose this topic not because he is applying for jobs or spending time on student platforms like my friends, but because he is more likely to face scams through phone calls, SMS messages, banking communication, customer-style fraud, and authority-based impersonation. Since he is older and also handles money and business interactions, I felt it was more useful to teach him about the psychology of scams rather than one narrow scam category. In other words, I wanted him to understand the pattern behind many scams, so that even if the exact message changes, he would still be able to recognise what is happening.

When I explained the topic, I focused on how scammers often succeed by making a person feel rushed, frightened, or pressured by someone who appears official. I taught him that urgency appears when a caller or message says things like “do this right now,” “your account will be blocked,” or “respond immediately.” I explained that fear is used when scammers claim that money is missing, a serious account problem has occurred, or some legal issue is about to happen. I also explained that authority is used when scammers pretend to be from a bank, the police, a mobile company, or a government office. I wanted him to see that many scams are not technically complicated at all. Instead, they are built to make the victim feel emotionally off-balance, so that they act before thinking clearly. That was the main teaching point of this activity: once you understand the emotional trick, you become much less likely to fall for the scam.

I also made sure to teach him what to do in practice when these situations happen. I told him that the first protective habit is to slow down and break the pressure cycle. If someone is demanding immediate action, that itself should be treated as a warning sign. I explained that he should never share PINs, passwords, one-time codes, or banking information with anyone who contacts him unexpectedly, no matter how official they sound. I also advised him that if someone claims to be from the bank or another authority, he should end the conversation and contact the real organisation directly using a trusted phone number from an official source. Because he is a small business owner, we also discussed how scammers may try to target people like him by sounding like suppliers, service providers, or financial authorities, which makes this kind of awareness even more important. After the teaching session, we had a deeper conversation about the cybersecurity situation in Bangladesh and how small business owners can sometimes be especially vulnerable because they are busy, handle transactions regularly, and may not always have strong awareness or formal protections in place. He asked me thoughtful questions about why scammers target ordinary people, how they make themselves sound believable, and what he should do if he ever becomes unsure about a call or message. That made the activity feel very meaningful to me, because it was not only a short lesson, but also a genuine discussion that helped connect cybersecurity awareness to the real risks people face in everyday life.
