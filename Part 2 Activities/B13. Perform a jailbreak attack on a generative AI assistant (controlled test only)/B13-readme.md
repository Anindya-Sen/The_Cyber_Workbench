# B13. Perform a jailbreak attack on a generative AI assistant (controlled test only).


For this activity, I carried out a controlled jailbreak test on a generative AI assistant to see whether a smaller open model could be manipulated into producing harmful content that it should normally refuse to provide. The model I used was “Qwen 2.5-1.5B-Instruct” inside the “PocketPal AI” app. I chose this because it was easily accessible and allowed me to test how well a lightweight model handled safety-sensitive prompts in practice. My goal was not to use the information in real life, but to see whether the model’s guardrails could be bypassed through prompt manipulation. I decided to test it using a clearly dangerous topic: I asked about how to create cyanide poison. At first, the model did not fully cooperate and in some places refused or responded in a limited way, but I continued using more carefully worded and calculated prompts to push it further.

What I found was that the model’s safety behaviour was inconsistent. According to the exported chat, the assistant initially refused at one point, but after further prompting it eventually produced unsafe poison-related content, including step-by-step harmful instructions, even though this was exactly the kind of content that should have remained blocked in the first place. This showed me that the model could be jailbroken through prompt manipulation, which is a serious weakness. In other words, the safeguard was not stable enough to hold its boundary once the conversation was reframed and pressured in different ways. That is what made this activity valuable from a cybersecurity point of view: it demonstrated that the model’s alignment could be weakened, and that a smaller free AI app can still become dangerous if its protective behaviour is not robust.

I found this especially concerning because the topic I used was not harmless or theoretical. It involved a high-risk poisoning subject, so the fact that the model eventually “cracked” and gave dangerous guidance shows how serious jailbreak vulnerabilities can be. A weak model like this does not just give wrong answers sometimes — it can also be manipulated into generating content that could cause real harm if placed in the wrong hands. That made me understand that jailbreak attacks are not only about making an AI act silly or ignore a rule for entertainment. They can expose deep safety problems in the model’s design and turn an easily available assistant into something genuinely risky. This is what made the test feel important and worth documenting: it clearly showed how prompt engineering can sometimes defeat built-in safety controls.

Overall, I observed the model’s behaviour across multiple prompts, and confirmed that the assistant could be pushed into producing content it should not have provided. As evidence, I kept the exported material from the app, including a PDF transcript of the chat and the original JSON export file, which record the conversation and show how the jailbreak developed over time. In my view, the main lesson from this activity is that even free and convenient AI tools can become dangerous when their safety protections are weak, and that jailbreak resistance is a very important part of building secure generative AI systems.
